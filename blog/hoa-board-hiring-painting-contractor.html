Nine questions an HOA board should ask before hiring a painter.

A community repaint is one of the largest checks most associations write. These are the questions that separate a real commercial contractor from a low bid that gets expensive later.

By Cal Pac Painting of Nevada · August 22, 2026

Why the bid packet is not enough

Most associations compare painting bids on two lines: the number and the color scheme. But the bids that go wrong rarely go wrong on price — they go wrong on scope, phasing and accountability. These nine questions surface the differences a bottom-line comparison hides.

The nine questions

  • 1. What is your Nevada contractor license number — and what is its bid limit? Both are public record on the Nevada State Contractors Board site. The bid limit matters more than boards expect: a contractor whose limit is below your contract value cannot legally take the job as one contract. (Cal Pac's is license #0044823 with a $750,000 limit — look it up rather than take anyone's word, ours included.)
  • 2. Exactly what preparation is included? Get it in writing: washing, crack repair, caulking removal and replacement, priming of bare spots. A bid that says "prep as needed" has told you nothing — and prep is where a cheap bid hides its savings.
  • 3. What specification does the work meet? Association repaints usually carry approved color schemes and product specs. Ask how the contractor documents compliance with government, builder and association specifications — and who signs off.
  • 4. How will the community be phased? Residents need to know when their building, their parking and their gates are affected. Ask for a zone map and a schedule the board can attach to its notices. A contractor who cannot produce one has not done many communities.
  • 5. Who is on site every day, and what do they record? There should be a named supervisor keeping a running record of job-site activity — not a crew that appears and disappears. Ask who the board's contact is when something comes up mid-job.
  • 6. What happens at the end of each day? The answer you want is that the site gets picked up daily. Communities are lived in; a repaint should not mean six weeks of drop cloths and cans in the common areas.
  • 7. What does the final inspection look like? A multi-point inspection with a board walk-through beats "we'll drive by." Ask how punch-list items get logged and closed.
  • 8. What warranty stands behind the work — and who answers the phone? A workmanship warranty is only as good as the company's reachability. Ask for the warranty term in writing and the direct line you call in month eleven.
  • 9. How long have your foremen been with you? Painting is a high-turnover trade. Crew tenure is the single best proxy for whether the crew that impressed you in the bid meeting is the crew that shows up.

The pattern in all nine: good commercial painters run on paperwork and named people. If the answers are vague, the job will be too.

What a good answer sounds like

You are not listening for salesmanship — you are listening for specifics. A license number you can verify tonight. A prep scope with nouns in it. A phasing map with dates. A supervisor with a name. A warranty with a term and a phone number. Contractors who work for boards and property managers produce these without being pushed, because their repeat business depends on the manager being able to defend the choice afterwards.
